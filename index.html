Research at the intersection of behavior, movement, and infectious disease

I study how animal social behavior and movement shape parasite exposure, infection risk, and health outcomes in wildlife. My work combines behavioral ecology, disease ecology, and long-term field data to understand how individual interactions scale up to population-level processes.

I am a fifth-year Ph.D. student in Ecology and Evolutionary Biology at Yale, advised by Vanessa Ezenwa. Much of my dissertation centers on African buffalo, where I investigate how social dynamics influence bovine tuberculosis and gastrointestinal parasite outcomes, and how infection reshapes behavior in return.

Current focus

My current work examines how fine-scale social interactions, movement ecology, and parasite burdens combine to shape disease risk and broader host ecology in free-ranging wildlife populations.

Research Themes

The central question running through my work is how behavior mediates both the costs and consequences of infection.

Social interactions and disease risk

I investigate how contact structure, social ties, and group-level organization influence who becomes exposed, infected, and affected.

Movement ecology and parasite transmission

I study how movement decisions connect hosts to infectious environments and how mobility shapes the spread of disease across space.

Feedbacks between infection and behavior

I am interested in how parasites alter host behavior and physiology, and how those changes ripple outward to reshape social and ecological dynamics.
